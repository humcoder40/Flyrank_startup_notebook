Predicting Content Decay in Organic Search: A Baseline vs. ML Validation Framework

Lead Developer: Humayun Naseem Company: FlyRank AI Role: Machine Learning Intern Unit of Analysis: content_hash_id

Abstract

Can a supervised classifier actually beat a two line heuristic at flagging decaying content for editorial review, or does it just look like it can. I built both against a 30,000 page development slice pulled with DuckDB from a 519,606 row FlyRank search warehouse, using only 90 day trailing Google Search Console signals. The heuristic, flag anything older than 180 days with more than 500 impressions, scored 0.360 precision and 0.201 recall on held out data. The first Random Forest I trained scored 1.000 precision and 1.000 recall, which is not really a result, it is more of a bug report. Auditing the feature matrix turned up six leaky columns, one of which (future_clicks_30d) was basically the answer key. After removing every leaky feature and re running validation on a client grouped split so the model could not just memorize per domain traffic baselines, precision fell to exactly 0.000. The honest version of the model has no signal left to work with. The baseline heuristic, dumb as it is, is the only thing in this project that actually works, and it is what shipped.

Introduction and Industry Problem Statement

Organic search is one of the only acquisition channels that keeps paying out after you stop spending on it. A page that ranks well can sit there converting traffic for years without another dollar of ad spend behind it. The catch is that nothing in search stays still. Competitors publish newer, more complete pages, Google reworks its ranking signals on a rolling basis, and increasingly the SERP itself gets shorter. AI Overviews and featured snippets eat the top of the page before a user ever scrolls to an organic result. The combined effect on any given page is what SEO teams call content decay, a slow bleed of rankings, impressions, and clicks that is not always obvious until the traffic is already gone.

FlyRank runs SEO operations across a large multi client portfolio, hundreds of thousands of URLs, spread across domains with very different traffic profiles. The editorial team doing the actual rewrites is small relative to that portfolio, so the real constraint is not knowing that decay happens, it is deciding which handful of pages out of thousands are worth an editor's time this week. Get that ranking wrong and you either burn editor hours refreshing pages that were fine, or let genuinely decaying pages sit untouched until the traffic loss shows up in a revenue report, by which point it is a much bigger fix.

Most teams solve this with a hand written rule, flag anything old and popular enough to matter, ignore everything else. It works, it is instant to compute, and anyone on the team can explain it in one sentence. It is also blind to interaction effects. It cannot tell the difference between a page that is stale because nobody has touched it and a page that is stale because it simply does not need touching, and it treats normal seasonal dips the same as structural decline.

This project, my Lane 2 assignment for the FlyRank ML internship, was a straightforward question dressed up as a research project. Given the trailing search signals FlyRank already collects, does a supervised model actually beat that rule, or does the rule hold up. I went in expecting the model to win by some modest margin. What actually happened was more useful than that, and is basically the whole reason this write up exists.

Data Architecture and Exploratory Analysis

The source data lives in the FlyRank ML internship warehouse as a set of Parquet tables, dim_content.parquet being the relevant one here, totaling 519,606 rows. I pulled the working slice with DuckDB rather than loading the full warehouse into memory, since 44 columns across half a million rows is more than enough to make iterative experimentation painful on a laptop. The dev sample used for every result in this paper is 30,000 rows, each one a single pseudonymized content asset keyed on content_hash_id. Client domains, URLs, page titles, and raw query strings were hashed or stripped at ingestion. I never had access to which real client or page any row corresponded to, which is by design, and also, as it turns out, made a few of the sanity checks below harder than they needed to be.

Extraction and Feature Contract

Before touching any model, I went column by column through the 44 available fields and separated what is actually knowable at decision time from what is not. This matters more than it sounds like it should. A feature can be perfectly real and still be useless, or actively dangerous, if it was not actually available on the day an editor would have needed the prediction. Five features cleared that bar cleanly:

  • content_age_days, knowable at the decision moment (historical publish date, no ambiguity).
  • impressions_90d, knowable at the decision moment (historical Search Console logs, backward looking window).
  • clicks_90d, knowable at the decision moment (same historical trailing traffic basis).
  • ctr_90d, knowable at the decision moment (a ratio of the two features above, so it inherits their validity).
  • avg_position, knowable at the decision moment (past SERP rank, logged historically by GSC).

Everything else in the 44 column set either encoded some form of forward looking window, or was derived, directly or indirectly, from the label itself. That distinction ends up being the entire second half of this paper, so I will come back to it in detail in the audit section.

Methodology and Algorithmic Formulation

Target Variable

The label, is_declining_label, is a direct pass through of trend_direction == "down" as already computed in the warehouse. I did not engineer this from scratch. It is a proxy for genuine structural decay rather than decay itself, and it is worth flagging up front, because it becomes directly relevant to the leakage story below.

Baseline Heuristic

The rule I used as the performance floor is deliberately dumb:

# The Operational Baseline Triage Heuristic
if content_age_days > 180 and impressions_90d > 500:
    reason_code = "STALE_HIGH_DEMAND"
    action_label = "PRIORITY_REFRESH"
    return True
else:
    return False

Evaluated as a ranked queue against held out data (Precision@50, meaning precision on the top 50 highest priority flagged candidates, which is closer to how an editorial team would actually consume this list than raw precision across every flagged row), it landed at 0.360 precision, 0.201 recall. Low recall is fine here. The rule is explicitly conservative by design, and a triage tool that misses some decaying pages is a much smaller problem than one that sends editors chasing false positives.

Random Forest Architecture

The candidate model is a Random Forest classifier, bagged decision trees, each trained on a bootstrap resample of the training set, with the feature space randomly subset, roughly the square root of the total feature count, at every split to decorrelate the trees and control variance. Each split minimizes Gini impurity:

G  =  1  −  ∑k=1C (pk)2

where p_k is the empirical fraction of class k at that node. Final predictions come from majority vote across the forest. I did not do an exhaustive hyperparameter sweep for this assignment. The point of the exercise was never to squeeze out an extra point of precision, it was to test the baseline vs. ML question honestly, so I used a reasonably regularized default (bounded tree depth, balanced class weighting) rather than letting the trees grow unconstrained and overfit on top of everything else that turned out to be wrong with the pipeline.

The Feature Leakage Audit

The first training run, on a plain random 80/20 split with all 44 columns available to the model, came back at 1.000 precision and 1.000 recall. That is not a good result, it is a red flag. A model that never misses on volatile, real world web traffic data means it is not predicting anything, it is reading the label off a feature that was never supposed to be there.

Going column by column through the feature matrix turned up six features that should not have been in there, falling into two categories:

  • The forward looking trap, future_clicks_30d. This column tracked clicks in the 30 days after the decision point. It ended up in the feature set by accident during the initial ETL join. Injecting it and re training in isolation confirmed it on its own. Model accuracy jumped straight to about 99 percent the moment it was added, because the model was not predicting decay, it was reading next month's numbers.
  • Target derived proxies, trend_pct and four downstream derivatives (trend_pct_30d_lag, momentum_score, decay_velocity, label_source_confidence). These are all variables computed at some stage from the same month over month comparison that trend_direction, and therefore the label itself, was built from. Feeding these to the model is not prediction, it is letting the model reverse engineer the labeling formula.

That is six leaky columns total, out of 44. Removing them was necessary but not, on its own, sufficient. There was still a validation level problem to deal with.

Validation Protocol: Grouped vs. Random Splits

A standard random 80/20 split lets rows from the same client end up in both train and test. With enough rows per client, a model can partially solve the task just by memorizing that Client A runs high traffic pages and Client B does not, without learning anything about decay behavior at all. I re ran validation with GroupShuffleSplit on client_id so every client's pages land entirely in train or entirely in test, never both, forcing the model to generalize to domains it has never seen.

What is worth showing explicitly is that I ran the grouped split as its own test before removing the leaky features, specifically to see how much of the 1.000 score was coming from split leakage versus feature leakage:

Split StrategyLeaky Features Present?PrecisionRecall
Random 80/20Yes1.0001.000
Grouped (client aware)Yes1.0000.982
Grouped (client aware)No, purged0.0000.000

That middle row is the important one, and it is the part I would have missed without checking. Fixing the split alone barely moved the number, recall dipped from 1.000 to 0.982, precision did not move at all. If client level memorization had been the dominant issue, grouping the split should have hurt the score a lot more than that. It did not, which tells you the leaky features, not the split strategy, were doing almost all of the work in that first 1.000 score. Only once both problems were fixed together, leaky columns purged and client grouped validation, did the real number show up. Precision and recall both collapsed to 0.000, with scikit learn throwing an UndefinedMetricWarning because the model, stripped of everything it had been cheating with, stopped predicting the positive class at all.

Limitations and Honest Framing

The headline result here is not "machine learning does not work for content decay." It is narrower than that. On this specific 30,000 row slice, restricted to five leakage free trailing GSC features, a Random Forest has nothing to add over a two line rule. That is still a useful thing to know, but it is worth being precise about why the ceiling is where it is rather than treating 0.000 as some kind of final verdict on ML for this problem.

Three specific gaps in the underlying data, straight from the data limits notes in the notebook:

  • Unbalanced history. Not every content asset has the same amount of trailing history available. Newer pages have thinner 90 day windows than pages that have been live for years, which means the feature values are not strictly comparable across the sample.
  • GSC only early rows. Early records in the warehouse are sourced exclusively from Search Console, before other data sources were wired in, so older rows are structurally thinner than recent ones regardless of the content itself.
  • Window overlaps. The 90 day trailing windows for adjacent snapshots overlap rather than being cleanly partitioned, which introduces some autocorrelation between rows that a fully rigorous setup would need to account for.

On top of those three, there is the more obvious structural gap. Everything here is GSC only. It tells you what happened on Google's own results pages and nothing about the things that actually cause a lot of real world decay, competitors publishing something better, backlink profiles shifting, or SERP real estate getting eaten by AI Overviews. A model that could see those signals might well beat the baseline, this one cannot, because they are simply not in the data it has access to.

Ranked Action Playbook

Since the honest model has no edge over the heuristic, the playbook that actually shipped is the heuristic, with the ML work kept as a documented dead end rather than something bolted onto production.

  • Step 1, deploy the baseline queue. baseline_action_score.csv is the operational output, every row tagged STALE_HIGH_DEMAND / PRIORITY_REFRESH, ready for the editorial team to pull directly.
  • Step 2, rank by impressions, not age. Given how skewed the demand distribution is (Signal 2 above), the queue is force sorted by impressions_90d descending, so the highest traffic stale pages surface first regardless of how far past the 180 day cutoff they are.
  • Step 3, human SERP check before any rewrite. For at least the top 20 candidates, an editor checks the live SERP before touching anything, mainly to catch cases where the real cause is an AI Overview or featured snippet eating the click, which no amount of content rewriting fixes.
  • Step 4, no autonomous CMS writes. Nothing in this pipeline, heuristic or model, gets wired directly into the CMS to edit, redirect, or remove pages without a human in the loop. A false positive here means deleting or de indexing a page that was actually fine, which is a far more expensive mistake than a missed refresh.

Showcase Walkthrough

The internal demo for this project runs in five minutes, in this order. First, show the baseline queue and its 0.360 precision as the floor. Second, show the first Random Forest run at 1.000 precision and ask the room if that seems believable. Third, walk through the six leaky columns and the future_clicks_30d injection test that isolates the problem. Fourth, show the grouped split with leakage still present row from the results table, to make the point that fixing the split alone was not enough. Fifth, land on the honest 0.000 and the resulting decision to ship the heuristic. The order matters, leading with the failure would undersell how convincing the wrong answer looked first.
